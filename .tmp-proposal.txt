ABSTRACT  
 
Communication barriers between Deaf sign language users and hearing individuals remain a 
persistent global accessibility challenge with profound social, educational, and economic 
consequences. In Sri Lanka, over 35,000 individuals are profoundly Deaf [2], yet the absence 
of any real-time automated translation between Sinhala spoken language and Sinhala Sign 
Language (SSL) forces Deaf Sri Lankans to rely on scarce human interpreters or remain 
excluded from spontaneous verbal interaction entirely. 
 
Existing systems such as Google Live Transcribe, Ava, and academic sign language 
production models either target text-only output, operate exclusively in English with 
American Sign Language (ASL), or produce only static pre-rendered animations with no 
prosodic or emotional conditioning. None address the linguistic specifics of Sinhala — an 
agglutinative language with complex morphological inflection — nor the absence of any 
public Sinhala-to-SSL parallel corpus. 
 
This proposal presents the Speech-to-Sign Linguistic and Prosodic Processing Component of 
Research Project R26-SE-019, which delivers real-time Sinhala spoken audio to SSL 
translation. The proposed system introduces a novel Unified Emotion-Conditioned Sign 
Synthesis Transformer — a single mT5-based encoder-decoder with dual output heads that 
jointly generates SSL gloss token sequences and 3D bone rotation parameters at 30fps from 
Sinhala text input. This replaces the fragmented two-model approach of the closest prior 
work (Gamage et al., ICAC 2023) with a unified architecture capable of real-time, 
emotionally modulated, anatomically correct SSL signing. 
 
A critical and architecturally unique stage is Sinhala morphological normalization using 
sinling, which converts agglutinative Sinhala word forms to SSL-compatible base forms 
before gloss mapping. Emotion classification is performed using XLM-RoBERTa, confirmed 
by Dhananjaya et al. (LREC 2022) as the best model for Sinhala NLP tasks, while prosodic 
features are extracted via librosa. An original Sinhala-to-SSL parallel corpus of 500+ 
sentence pairs will be co-constructed with Deaf signers at the School for the Deaf, Ratmalana 
— the first such corpus to exist. 
 
The system streams bone animation data via FastAPI WebSocket to an Expo Android 
application rendering a live 3D signing avatar using Three.js and native OpenGL ES via 
expo-gl. Expected outcomes include Sinhala ASR WER below 10%, emotion classification 
accuracy above 85%, BLEU-4 above 0.40, bone regression MSE below 0.05 radians, end-to-
[[PAGE 4]]
4 
 
end latency below 500ms, and Deaf user sign comprehension above 80%. The system 
directly advances SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities) by enabling genuine real-time inclusion for 
the Sri Lankan Deaf community in healthcare, education, and public service. 
Keywords: Sinhala Sign Language, SSL, Whisper ASR, XLM-RoBERTa, mT5, sinling, 
Morphological Normalization, Bone Animation, Emotion-Conditioned Synthesis, Real-Time

1. INTRODUCTION 
1.1 Background and Context 
1.1.1 The Global and Local Scale of the Problem 
Communication is a fundamental human right enshrined in the Universal Declaration of 
Human Rights and reaffirmed in the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons 
with Disabilities (UNCRPD, 2006) [5]. Yet an estimated 70 million Deaf individuals 
worldwide [1] rely on sign language as their primary means of expression in a world 
overwhelmingly oriented towards spoken and written language. The resulting communication 
chasm manifests across every dimension of daily life — education, healthcare, employment, 
legal services, and social participation — with consequences that are simultaneously 
measurable and unjust. 
In Sri Lanka, the scale of this exclusion is acute. Government health surveys estimate that 
over 35,000 individuals are profoundly Deaf [2], with a substantially larger group 
experiencing significant hearing loss. Despite this, fewer than 3% of public service providers 
— including hospitals, banks, courts, and government offices — employ staff with any 
Sinhala Sign Language (SSL) proficiency [2]. A Deaf Sri Lankan seeking medical attention, 
pursuing legal recourse, or accessing government services faces systematic exclusion from 
the communication infrastructure of their own society. 
The economic consequences compound the social harm. The International Labor 
Organization (ILO) reports that persons with disabilities, including Deaf individuals, earn on 
average 25–30% less than hearing counterparts in comparable roles, with significantly lower 
workforce participation rates [4]. In Sri Lanka, the cost of engaging a professional SSL 
interpreter — where one is even available — ranges from LKR 200,000 to LKR 400,000 
annually per Deaf employee, placing the burden of inclusion almost entirely on either the 
employer or the individual. These structural barriers directly violate the spirit and letter of 
UNCRPD Article 21, which mandates that state parties ensure full accessibility of 
communication in all public domains. 
Educational exclusion begins early. Deaf students in mainstream Sri Lankan schools 
frequently lack access to real-time SSL interpretation during lessons, resulting in missed 
instruction, reduced comprehension, and lower academic attainment. The Sri Lanka School 
for the Deaf, Ratmalana — the primary institution serving Deaf children in the country — 
serves fewer than 400 students, leaving the majority of Deaf children in Sri Lanka without 
specialized linguistic support. The downstream effects on employment, civic participation, 
and mental health are well documented [3]. 
"The exclusion of Deaf communities from digital communication tools is not a minor accessibility 
gap — it is a systemic denial of the right to participate in society." — World Federation of the 
Deaf, 2023 [1] 
[[PAGE 13]]
13 
 
1.1.2 The Technology Gap 
Assistive technology for Deaf communities has advanced significantly in the speech-to-text 
domain over the past decade. Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems such as Google 
Live Transcribe, OpenAI Whisper, and Microsoft Azure Speech Services now achieve near-
human accuracy on English speech in controlled conditions. However, the fundamental 
modality mismatch between these tools and sign language communication remains entirely 
unaddressed for Sinhala: these systems produce text, not sign language, and they are not 
designed for the linguistic structures, prosodic cues, or visual-gestural grammar of SSL. 
Sign language is not a manual encoding of spoken language. SSL has its own grammar, word 
order (topic-comment structure rather than subject-verb-object), spatial referencing system, 
and a rich non-manual component in which facial expressions, head movements, and body 
posture carry grammatical meaning. A system that merely transcribes Sinhala speech to text 
and displays it on screen provides some accessibility but fails to bridge communication to 
Deaf individuals whose primary language is SSL and who may have limited written Sinhala 
literacy. A genuine communication bridge requires translation to SSL — including its full 
grammatical and prosodic expressiveness. 
No such system currently exists for Sinhala. The research community has produced sign 
language production systems for English-to-ASL translation, but these are fundamentally 
inapplicable to the Sri Lankan context: they use English NLP pipelines, English-ASL parallel 
corpora such as PHOENIX-14T, and English-specific language models that produce only 
[UNK] tokens for Sinhala Unicode script. Sinhala presents additional unique challenges as an 
agglutinative language — a single verb root may appear in over 40 inflected surface forms 
depending on tense, person, number, and case — yet no prior sign language production 
system includes a Sinhala morphological preprocessing stage. 
 
1.1.3 Proposed Solution Overview 
This research project proposes a real-time end-to-end pipeline for Sinhala Audio to Sinhala 
Sign Language (SSL) translation, delivered as an Android application for Deaf users. The 
system architecture comprises six sequential processing stages: (1) Sinhala ASR via fine-
tuned Whisper, (2a) prosody extraction via librosa, (2b) Sinhala morphological normalization 
via sinling, (3) emotion classification via XLM-RoBERTa, (4) unified mT5 synthesis 
transformer generating SSL glosses and 3D bone rotations, and (5–6) real-time avatar 
rendering via FastAPI WebSocket and Expo Android app. 
The central novel contribution is the Unified Emotion-Conditioned Sign Synthesis 
Transformer: a single mT5-based model with dual output heads that jointly learn SSL gloss 
generation and anatomically correct bone rotation regression from a shared Sinhala encoder, 
conditioned on prosody and emotion vectors. This eliminates the need for a lookup table of 
pre-recorded signing clips, enabling the system to generate novel SSL signing for any Sinhala 
input in real time, with emotional expressiveness modulated by the speaker's detected tone. 
[[PAGE 14]]
14 
 
The system will be validated by a Deaf user comprehension study at the School for the Deaf, 
Ratmalana, and targets: Sinhala WER below 10%, emotion accuracy above 85%, SSL BLEU-
4 above 0.40, bone MSE below 0.05 rad, and end-to-end latency below 500ms. The Android 
APK will sustain 25fps avatar rendering on target hardware. 
This project contributes to SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities) and aligns with the SLIIT Centre 
of Excellence for AI (CoEAI) research cluster on human-computer interaction and assistive 
technology.

Several consistent limitations emerge across all commercial systems. First, every system 
produces text or pre-rendered American Sign Language output — none support SSL or 
Sinhala in any form. Second, all systems are fundamentally unidirectional: they assist Deaf 
individuals in understanding spoken language but do not generate sign language output that 
allows hearing individuals to communicate through a Deaf-accessible visual modality. Third, 
no system incorporates prosodic or emotional features from the source speech, despite the 
established linguistic significance of non-manual markers (NMMs) in sign languages, where 
facial expressions encode questions, negation, conditional clauses, and emotional intensity. 
Fourth, all systems require internet connectivity and cloud processing, introducing latency 
incompatible with natural conversational rhythm. 
 
Critical limitation across all commercial systems: zero support for Sinhala audio input, zero 
support for Sinhala Sign Language output, and zero prosodic or emotional conditioning of 
signing output. 
 
1.2.2 Academic Research Systems — Analysis and Limitations 
 
Academic research has advanced sign language production significantly beyond commercial 
offerings, particularly through the adoption of transformer architectures. This section reviews 
the most relevant works, tracing the progression from rule-based to deep learning approaches 
and identifying the gaps each leaves. 
Ko et al. [10] proposed one of the earliest neural sign language production systems using 
sequence-to-sequence transformer models for English-to-ASL gloss translation. Their work 
demonstrated that transformer encoder-decoder architectures could learn the structural 
mappings between English grammar and ASL grammar, including reordering of subject-verb-
object to topic-comment structure. However, the system produced only gloss sequences — 
symbolic representations of signs — rather than motion parameters, and required a separate 
rendering system to produce avatar animation. The system was trained entirely on 
[[PAGE 16]]
16 
 
PHOENIX-14T, an English-German sign language corpus, making it entirely inapplicable to 
Sinhala or SSL. 
Stoll et al. [11] extended sign language production by introducing prosody-awareness, 
demonstrating that incorporating pitch, energy, and rhythm features from source audio 
improved the naturalness and comprehensibility of generated sign sequences. This work 
provided the theoretical and empirical foundation for including prosodic features in the 
present pipeline. However, Stoll et al. operated on English speech only, used a generative 
adversarial network (GAN) for animation rather than direct bone regression, and produced 
only upper-body pose sequences rather than full anatomical bone rotations compatible with 
3D avatar frameworks. 
Saunders et al. [12] introduced the Progressive Transformer, specifically designed for sign 
language production, which improved temporal consistency of generated signing sequences 
by producing motion parameters progressively rather than in a single pass. Their model 
achieved state-of-the-art BLEU-4 scores on PHOENIX-14T and demonstrated that 
transformer architectures substantially outperform recurrent approaches for sign language 
production. However, their system was again English-ASL only, produced 2D joint positions 
rather than 3D Euler angles, and had no real-time inference capability — inference time per 
sentence was approximately 2–4 seconds on GPU hardware. 
Camgoz et al. [13] produced the first end-to-end neural sign language translation system, 
combining a CNN feature extractor for signing videos with a transformer sequence-to-
sequence model for gloss and translation generation. This established the benchmark 
architecture for sign language translation but was designed for recognition (sign-to-text) 
rather than production (text-to-sign), and operated on PHOENIX-14T exclusively. 
For ASR, Radford et al.'s Whisper model [14] represents the current state of the art in 
multilingual speech transcription. Trained on 680,000 hours of multilingual audio, Whisper 
achieves robust performance across 99 languages including Sinhala (language code 'si'), with 
strong noise resilience and speaker-independent accuracy. Critically, Whisper is the only 
publicly available ASR model with confirmed Sinhala language support at a quality level 
suitable for downstream NLP processing, making it the only viable choice for Stage 1 of the 
proposed pipeline. 
For Sinhala NLP, Dhananjaya et al. [19] provided the most directly relevant prior work 
through their comprehensive evaluation of pre-trained language models on Sinhala 
downstream tasks. They demonstrated conclusively that XLM-RoBERTa outperforms 
multilingual BERT, Sinhala BERT, and all English-only models on Sinhala classification 
tasks including sentiment and emotion detection. Critically, they showed that English-only 
models such as RoBERTa-base produce only [UNK] tokens for Sinhala Unicode input, 
making them entirely unsuitable for the present application. This work directly determines 
the model selection for Stage 3 of the proposed pipeline. 
Gamage et al. [20] produced the closest prior work to the present research — a Sinhala Sign 
Language translation system using deep learning. Their ICAC 2023 paper presents a system 
[[PAGE 17]]
17 
 
using two separate CNN models: a face emotion classifier operating on the speaker's video, 
and a sign selection model mapping detected emotion and speech content to pre-recorded 
SSL GIF animations. While Gamage et al. demonstrate the feasibility of automated SSL 
production, their system exhibits five critical limitations that the present work directly 
addresses: (1) it requires video of the speaker's face rather than processing audio-derived 
emotion; (2) it produces only static pre-rendered GIF animations rather than real-time bone 
animation; (3) the two separate CNNs cannot share learned representations between emotion 
and sign generation; (4) there is no Sinhala ASR stage — text input is assumed; and (5) there 
is no morphological preprocessing for Sinhala's agglutinative inflection.

1.2.3 Sri Lankan and Regional Contributions 
Within Sri Lanka, a small number of research efforts have addressed sign language 
accessibility, though all remain confined to educational or static use cases. Wanasinghe et al. 
[17] developed Hastha, an online learning platform for the hearing impaired that presents 
SSL signs through instructional video content. While pedagogically valuable, Hastha is a one-
directional learning tool with no automated translation capability. The Sinhala Sign Language 
[[PAGE 18]]
18 
 
Learning System [18] similarly provides structured SSL education through a web interface 
but does not process natural speech input or produce real-time signing output. 
Both systems serve a genuine need for SSL education but confirm rather than address the 
research gap: there is no system that can take spontaneous Sinhala speech as input and 
produce real-time, expressive SSL animation output. The absence of even a Sinhala-SSL 
parallel corpus further underscores the lack of foundational research infrastructure in this 
domain, making corpus construction itself a primary research contribution of the present 
project. 
1.2.4 Existing Solution Limitations — Synthesis 
Across the full spectrum of commercial and academic systems reviewed, six categories of 
limitation consistently emerge that the proposed system directly addresses: 
• Modality gap: All commercial systems output text, not sign language. Deaf users 
whose primary language is SSL are not served by text captioning alone, particularly 
those with limited written Sinhala literacy. 
• Language specificity: All sign language production research uses English-ASL or 
German-DGS pairs. No system exists for Sinhala-to-SSL, and English NLP models 
are demonstrably non-functional on Sinhala Unicode input. 
• Morphological blindness: Sinhala's agglutinative morphology means inflected forms 
cannot be directly mapped to SSL glosses. No prior sign language system includes a 
Sinhala morphological preprocessing stage. 
• Prosody and emotion absence: Non-manual markers (NMMs) — facial expressions, 
head movements, body posture — are grammatically essential in SSL. No existing 
Sinhala sign language system incorporates prosodic or emotional features from source 
speech. 
• Static animation: The only prior Sinhala SSL system (Gamage et al., 2023) produces 
static pre-rendered GIF animations from a fixed library, precluding real-time 
generation of novel signing sequences for arbitrary input. 
• Corpus vacuum: No public Sinhala-to-SSL parallel corpus exists, making supervised 
training of any data-driven Sinhala SSL system impossible without first constructing 
the corpus — a significant barrier that this project addresses directly.

1.3.2 Novelty and Justification 
The proposed system addresses all four gaps simultaneously through a single integrated 
architecture. The novelty is not incremental — it is architectural. Rather than adapting an 
existing English sign language production system to Sinhala, this work designs from the 
ground up for Sinhala's specific linguistic characteristics and the SSL community's specific 
needs. 
The central novel contribution — the Unified Emotion-Conditioned Sign Synthesis 
Transformer — has no direct precedent in the literature. While multi-task transformers are 
well-established in NLP, no prior work applies a multi-task transformer to jointly predict sign 
language gloss sequences and anatomically parameterized 3D bone rotations from a minority 
language input with emotion conditioning. The dual-head mT5 architecture enables the model 
to learn cross-modal representations between Sinhala linguistic structure and SSL motion 
[[PAGE 20]]
20 
 
parameters — something impossible with the lookup-table approach of Gamage et al. [20] or 
the separate-model approach of all prior sign language production systems. 
The sinling morphological normalization stage represents a contribution to the NLP 
preprocessing pipeline for low-resource agglutinative languages in sign language contexts. 
No prior work has addressed the specific challenge of mapping agglutinative Sinhala surface 
forms to sign language glosses, and the approach developed here — stemming to base form, 
extracting tense markers as SSL temporal adverb annotations, and converting case suffixes to 
SSL spatial grammar markers — provides a principled linguistic framework applicable to 
other agglutinative languages in future work. 
The original Sinhala-SSL parallel corpus, constructed in collaboration with the School for the 
Deaf, Ratmalana using MediaPipe and Kalidokit, will be the first such resource to exist 
publicly. Its creation constitutes a research contribution independent of the translation system 
itself, providing a foundation for future Sinhala SSL research by other researchers. 
 
This research is the first to simultaneously address all four identified gaps in Sinhala-to-SSL 
translation: real-time pipeline, morphological preprocessing, prosody/emotion conditioning, and 
original corpus construction. The combination of these contributions constitutes a novel and 
significant advance beyond the state of the art.

2. OBJECTIVE 
2.1 Main Objective 
To design and implement a real-time Sinhala Audio to Sinhala Sign Language (SSL) 
Translation Component that accurately transcribes Sinhala spoken input, normalizes Sinhala 
morphology, detects emotion and prosody, and generates expressive SSL signing via a 3D 
bone-animated avatar on an Android application, as part of the integrated R26-SE-019 
transformer-based communication system for Deaf–hearing interaction. 
2.2 Specific Objectives 
 
1. Implement a Sinhala ASR pipeline using Whisper-medium fine-tuned on Common 
V oice 13 Sinhala, achieving WER below 10%. 
 
2. Develop a Sinhala morphological normalization module using sinling to convert 
inflected Sinhala forms to SSL-compatible base forms, enabling correct gloss 
mapping for agglutinative word variants. 
 
 
3. Build a prosody and emotion analysis pipeline using librosa (F0, RMS, MFCCs) and 
XLM-RoBERTa (6-class Sinhala emotion classifier), achieving emotion accuracy 
above 85%. 
 
4. Design and train a Unified Emotion-Conditioned Sign Synthesis Transformer (mT5-
small) with dual output heads: gloss token generation and 3D bone rotation regression 
(21 bones × 3 Euler angles per frame). 
 
 
5. Construct an original Sinhala-to-SSL parallel corpus of 500+ sentence pairs at the 
School for the Deaf, Ratmalana, using Media Pipe and Kalid kit for ground-truth bone 
capture. 
 
6. Deliver a FastAPI WebSocket endpoint streaming bone JSON at 30fps to an Expo 
Android application rendering a live 3D SSL avatar at 25fps+ using expo-gl and 
Three.js, with end-to-end latency below 500ms. 
 
 
7. Validate the system with a Deaf user comprehension study achieving at least 80% 
correct sign identification.

3. METHODOLOGY 
3.1 Research Approach 
This project follows Design Science Research (DSR) methodology (Hevner et al., 2004), 
which is the established framework for research that creates and evaluates innovative IT 
artefacts as its primary contribution. DSR is appropriate here because the central outputs of 
the research are the artefacts themselves — the Sinhala-SSL corpus, the fine-tuned ASR 
model, the unified synthesis transformer, and the Android avatar application — rather than 
theoretical propositions about the social phenomenon being addressed. 
The DSR cycle applied in this project consists of five phases: (1) Problem Identification — 
systematic review of existing systems and identification of the four research gaps (completed 
in Chapter 1); (2) Objectives Definition — specification of measurable performance targets 
for each pipeline stage (Chapter 2); (3) Design and Development — iterative construction 
and testing of each pipeline stage (Chapter 3); (4) Demonstration — integration of all stages 
and end-to-end system demonstration; and (5) Evaluation — quantitative metric evaluation 
and Deaf user comprehension study (Section 3.7). 
The iterative nature of DSR is particularly appropriate for the multi-stage pipeline of this 
project, where each stage's output quality directly affects all downstream stages. Stages 1 and 
2b (ASR and morphological normalization) will be developed and validated first, as the 
quality of Sinhala text normalization is a prerequisite for training the synthesis transformer. 
The corpus construction (Stage 4 objective) runs in parallel with ASR development to 
maximise the data available for transformer training. 
 
3.2 Pipeline Architecture 
The pipeline comprises six sequential processing stages. All stages are Sinhala-specific where 
required or language-agnostic where the processing operates on acoustic waveforms or 
geometric data independent of language. The critical architectural distinction from all prior 
Sinhala sign language work is Stage 2b — Sinhala morphological normalisation — which has 
no equivalent in any published sign language pipeline for any language.

3.3 Stage 1 — Sinhala ASR (Whisper) 
The first stage of the pipeline converts raw Sinhala spoken audio into Unicode text with 
word-level timestamps. OpenAI Whisper-medium is selected as the base model for four 
reasons: (1) it is the only publicly available ASR model with confirmed support for Sinhala at 
sufficient quality for downstream NLP; (2) its encoder-decoder transformer architecture 
enables fine-tuning on domain-specific data; (3) its multilingual pre-training on 680,000 
hours of data provides robust noise tolerance essential for real-world deployment; and (4) it 
provides word-level timestamp alignment necessary for synchronizing bone animation frames 
with spoken syllables.

3.4 Stage 2b — Sinhala Morphological Normalization (Novel Stage) 
This stage is architecturally unique — no equivalent preprocessing step exists in any 
published sign language production pipeline for any language. Its necessity arises directly 
from Sinhala's agglutinative morphological structure, which creates a vocabulary explosion 
problem that would make training the synthesis transformer infeasible without normalization. 
3.4.1 The Agglutinative Problem 
In Sinhala, a single root verb or noun may appear in dozens of distinct surface forms 
depending on tense, aspect, person, number, case, and register. For example, the verb root 
'කනවා' (to eat) appears as 'කනවා' (present, neutral), 'කෑවා' (past), 'කාවි' (past, formal), 
'කන්නන්' (present, emphatic), 'කන්ට' (infinitive dative), 'කාල' (perfective participle), and 
[[PAGE 25]]
25 
 
many others. Each of these is a distinct surface token, but they all correspond to the same 
SSL gloss: EAT. 
Without normalization, the synthesis transformer's input vocabulary contains thousands of 
surface form tokens mapping to a far smaller number of SSL glosses. This creates severe data 
sparsity — each surface form appears far less frequently in the training corpus than the 
corresponding base form would — and forces the model to learn separate mappings for what 
are linguistically identical concepts. The morphological normalization stage addresses this 
directly by reducing all inflected forms to their base gloss able form before the text is passed 
to the synthesis transformer. 
Tool: sinling (pip install sinling) — open-source Sinhala-native NLP library providing tokenization 
and rule-based stemming for standard Sinhala morphological classes 
Stemming process: Step 1: tokenize Sinhala Unicode sentence into word tokens; Step 2: apply 
sinling stemmer to reduce each token to morphological base form; Step 3: extract tense markers 
(past/present/future) as separate SSL temporal adverb annotations; Step 4: extract case suffixes (ට 
dative, නේ genitive, විසින් instrumental) and map to SSL spatial grammar markers 
Example 1: 'නෙදරට ගිය' (went home, dative) → base: ['නෙදර', 'යනවා'] + tense: PAST + case: 
DATIVE-LOC → SSL gloss: HOME GO [past-marker] [location-right] 
Example 2: 'ඔහු කෑවා' (he ate) → base: ['ඔහු', 'කනවා'] + tense: PAST → SSL gloss: HE EAT [past-
marker] 
Fallback: If sinling confidence score below threshold (0.6), full inflected surface form is passed to 
transformer with OOV flag; exception dictionary maintained for SSL domain-specific vocabulary 
(signs related to health, education, numbers) 
 
3.5 Stage 3 — Emotion Classification (XLM-RoBERTa) 
The emotion classification stage assigns a 6-class emotion label and continuous intensity 
score to each Sinhala sentence processed by the pipeline. This output serves as a conditioning 
vector for the synthesis transformer, modulating the amplitude and expressiveness of 
generated bone rotations to reflect the detected emotional tone of the speaker. 
Model selection is determined by Dhananjaya et al.'s [19] comprehensive evaluation of pre-
trained language models on Sinhala: XLM-RoBERTa achieves F1 scores of 72–78% on 
Sinhala sentiment tasks, compared to 45–52% for multilingual BERT and effectively 0% for 
English-only models (RoBERTa-base, BERT-base) which produce [UNK] for all Sinhala 
input. XLM-RoBERTa's pre-training on 100 languages including Sinhala using a shared 
subword vocabulary of 250,000 tokens ensures that Sinhala Unicode characters are 
represented as meaningful embeddings rather than unknown tokens. 
Base model: FacebookAI/xlm-roberta-base — 278M parameters, 12-layer transformer, pre-trained on 
CommonCrawl in 100 languages including Sinhala 
Emotion classes: 6 classes: neutral / happy (සතුටු) / sad (දුක්) / angry (නකෝප) / surprised (ආශ්චර්ය) / 
fearful (බය)

Fine-tune data: Phase 1: GoEmotions dataset (English, 28 classes → mapped to 6) for label transfer; 
Phase 2: 500–1000 manually annotated Sinhala emotion sentences collected and labelled by two 
annotators with inter-annotator agreement measured by Cohen's kappa 
Output: emotion_class (string) + intensity_score (float 0.0–1.0, derived from softmax probability of 
predicted class) — both fed as conditioning inputs to mT5 encoder 
Parallel execution: Stage 3 runs in parallel with Stage 2b on separate CPU thread to minimise total

3.8.1 FastAPI WebSocket Endpoint 
The FastAPI endpoint serves as the bridge between the Python-based AI pipeline and the 
JavaScript-based Android application. A WebSocket connection is used (rather than REST) 
because it maintains a persistent bidirectional channel, enabling the server to push bone 
rotation frames to the client as they are generated autoregressively by the mT5 decoder — 
without waiting for the complete sentence to be processed. This streaming approach is 
essential for achieving sub-500ms latency. 
Endpoint: ws://[server]/sign-stream — accepts Sinhala audio chunks as binary WebSocket messages; 
returns bone rotation JSON frames as the model generates them 
Frame format: {frame_id: int, timestamp_ms: float, bones: {boneName: {x: float, y: float, z: float}, 
...}, gloss: string|null, emotion: string} 
Concurrency: uvicorn with asyncio event loop; each WebSocket connection handled as an async 
coroutine; model inference runs in a thread pool executor to avoid blocking the event loop 
Latency target: First bone frame returned within 500ms of audio capture start; subsequent frames at 
30fps steady state

4. HIGH-LEVEL SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE 
 
4.1 Component Overview 
The R26-SE-019 system is a four-component integrated platform for bidirectional Deaf–
hearing communication. This proposal covers Component 1: the Speech-to-Sign Linguistic 
and Prosodic Processing Component, which handles the Sinhala audio input to SSL signing 
output direction. Component 2 (3D avatar rendering and display, Chamara R M L K) receives 
bone animation streams from Component 1. Components 3 and 4 address the reverse 
direction (SSL recognition and Sinhala speech synthesis). 
Component 1 operates as a self-contained AI inference pipeline with a public WebSocket 
API, enabling it to serve multiple concurrent Deaf users simultaneously while remaining fully 
upgradeable without affecting the avatar rendering or reverse-direction components. 
 
4.2 System Architecture and Data Flow 
 
 
Figure 3: R26-SE-019 — Full System Architecture and Component Interactions 
 
The data flow through the system follows a linear progression from hearing user speech 
capture to Deaf user avatar display. The hearing user's Sinhala speech is captured at 16kHz 
mono PCM by expo-av within the Android application and immediately streamed over a 
secure WebSocket connection (WSS) to the FastAPI backend hosted on a cloud GPU 
instance. 
On the backend, the audio stream is processed in two parallel threads: Thread A runs the 
Whisper ASR model to produce Sinhala Unicode text with word timestamps, while Thread B 
runs librosa prosody extraction on the same audio buffer to produce the 9-dimensional 
prosody feature vector. When Thread A completes, the Sinhala text is passed synchronously 

[[PAGE 34]]
34 
 
through sinling morphological normalisation (Stage 2b) and then to XLM-RoBERTa for 
emotion classification (Stage 3). The normalised text, prosody vector, and emotion vector are 
then concatenated and passed to the mT5 synthesis transformer. 
The mT5 model generates output autoregressively. As each decoder timestep is completed, 
the corresponding bone rotation frame (63 floats) is immediately serialised as JSON and 
pushed to the Android client over the open WebSocket connection. The client applies each 
received bone frame to the VRM avatar skeleton using bone.quaternion.slerp() in the Three.js 
animation loop, producing continuous smooth signing motion that begins appearing within 
500ms of the hearing user starting to speak.

4.4 Scalability and Security 
The pipeline is containerised using Docker with a single Dockerfile that packages the Python 
AI environment (PyTorch, HuggingFace, sinling, FastAPI) and exposes the WebSocket 
endpoint on port 8000. Horizontal scaling is achieved by deploying multiple container 
replicas behind an nginx WebSocket-aware load balancer, with each container handling 
independent WebSocket sessions with no shared state. 
Security measures include: (1) WSS (TLS 1.3) for all WebSocket connections using Let's 
Encrypt certificates; (2) JWT token authentication for WebSocket connection establishment; 
(3) in-memory-only audio processing — no Sinhala audio is persisted to disk or logged; (4) 
rate limiting (5 concurrent WebSocket sessions per IP) to prevent resource exhaustion; (5) 
model files stored in read-only Docker volumes with no runtime modification capability. 
The modular architecture enables independent upgrading of each model component. If a 
superior Sinhala ASR model becomes available, it can replace Whisper without any changes 
to the morphological normalisation, emotion, or synthesis stages. The mT5 model can be 
retrained on an expanded corpus and hot-swapped via Docker image update without 
application downtime.

6. COMMERCIALIZATION PLAN 
6.1 Target Market and Customer Persona 
The commercialization strategy targets three distinct market segments with different 
purchasing behaviors and value propositions 
 
6.1.1 Institutional Market (Primary) 
The institutional market comprises Sri Lankan healthcare providers, educational institutions, 
and government agencies with legal obligations under the UNCRPD and national disability 
legislation to provide accessible communication. The primary buyer persona is an HR or 
accessibility manager at a hospital, school, or government department who currently pays 
LKR 200,000–400,000 annually for ad-hoc interpreter services and is actively seeking a 
scalable digital alternative. This segment values reliability, compliance documentation, and 
institutional pricing models. 
Key institutional targets include: the Sri Lanka Ministry of Health (500+ hospitals and 
clinics), the Ministry of Education (500+ state schools), the Department of Government 
Information, Sri Lanka Telecom, and Bank of Ceylon — all of which have public-facing 
service desks regularly accessed by Deaf Sri Lankans. 
 
6.1.2 Consumer Market (Secondary) 
The consumer segment comprises Deaf individuals and their hearing family members who 
would benefit from a personal communication tool. The primary buyer persona is a hearing 
parent of a Deaf child, or a hearing spouse, who currently relies on basic gesture-based 
communication or a family member who has self-taught limited SSL. This segment values 
ease of installation, offline capability, and one-time pricing. 
 
6.1.3 NGO and Government Grant Market 
Given the strong SDG alignment of the system, significant funding opportunity exists 
through international development organisations including UNDP Sri Lanka, USAID Asia 
Disability Innovation Fund, and the EU Horizon Accessibility Fund. The Sri Lanka National 
Secretariat for Persons with Disabilities administers annual grants for assistive technology 
development and deployment. 
 
[[PAGE 40]]
40 
 
6.2 Value Proposition 
The system delivers the first real-time, emotionally expressive, Sinhala-to-SSL 
communication bridge, eliminating the dependency on human interpreters in standard Sri 
Lankan interaction settings. Quantifiable value: a single institutional license at USD 
200/month eliminates LKR 200,000–400,000 in annual interpreter costs per Deaf employee 
(payback period under 3 months). Qualitative value: genuine Deaf inclusion in spontaneous 
Sinhala conversation — not text captioning, but sign language — delivered to the user's own 
Android device. 
 
6.3 Revenue Model 
 
• SaaS Institutional Subscription: USD 150–300/month per organisation; unlimited 
concurrent users within organisation; annual contract; includes SSL model updates as 
SSL corpus grows 
• API Licensing: USD 0.002 per signing session for third-party integration; targets 
telehealth platforms, e-government portals, and educational software vendors in Sri 
Lanka 
• Consumer Android App: one-time purchase LKR 1,500 (~USD 5) on Google Play 
Store; offline mode with pre-compiled compact model variant 
• Government/NGO Grants: SLIIT technology transfer agreement with 15% royalty; 
UNDP and USAID grant applications for free deployment in public hospitals and 
schools 
 
6.4 Competitive Advantage and Intellectual Property 
Three elements of the system constitute potential intellectual property protectable through 
patent or trade secret: (1) the Unified Emotion-Conditioned Sign Synthesis Transformer 
architecture — the dual-head mT5 with joint gloss and bone regression loss — which has no 
direct prior art; (2) the sinling-to-SSL morphological normalisation pipeline — the specific 
rules for mapping Sinhala tense markers and case suffixes to SSL grammatical annotations; 
and (3) the Sinhala-SSL parallel corpus itself, which as a curated dataset may be protectable 
as a database right under Sri Lankan intellectual property law. 
The system's regional specificity provides a competitive moat that global players cannot 
easily replicate: Microsoft, Google, and Meta do not have SSL linguists, partnerships with the 
School for the Deaf, Ratmalana, or a Sinhala-SSL training corpus. Replicating the corpus 
alone would require 12–18 months of data collection effort with qualified SSL signers.

Development cost is dominated by cloud GPU compute for model training and the data 
collection effort at the School for the Deaf. Post-development, ongoing costs are primarily 
API hosting and corpus expansion. The pricing strategy targets break-even within 6 months 
of commercial launch with 5 institutional subscribers at the USD 200/month entry tier.

Appendix B: Ethical Considerations Checklist 
All data collection involving human participants will comply with the SLIIT Research Ethics 
Policy and the Declaration of Helsinki. The following checklist will be completed before any 
data collection begins: 
• Ethics Application: Complete SLIIT Ethics Review Board application submitted in 
Week 1, including study protocol, participant information sheet, consent forms in 
Sinhala and English, data management plan, and risk assessment 
• Informed Consent: Written informed consent obtained from all Deaf participants in 
both Sinhala and English; consent forms reviewed by a qualified SSL interpreter to 
ensure full comprehension by Deaf participants 
• Right to Withdraw: All participants advised in writing and through SSL interpretation 
that they may withdraw from the study at any time without consequence or 
explanation required 
• Data Anonymisation: All recorded Sinhala audio and signing video files assigned 
anonymous participant IDs; no names, faces (beyond hands and arms for signing), or 
identifying information stored in the corpus 
• Data Security: All corpus data stored on SLIIT-provisioned encrypted institutional 
storage; access restricted to the research team; no data transferred to personal devices 
or external cloud services 
• Community Validation: All SSL gloss annotations reviewed by a qualified SSL 
annotator from the School for the Deaf, Ratmalana, to ensure accuracy and cultural 
appropriateness 
• Participant Compensation: Deaf signers compensated at LKR 500/session as approved 
by the Ethics Board; School for the Deaf administration formally involved as 
institutional partner 
• Data Retention and Deletion: All personal data deleted within 6 months of final 
project submission; anonymised corpus data retained and published with Ethics Board 
approval for research community benefit

Appendix C: Sinhala Sign Language — Linguistic Background 
C.1 SSL Grammar Overview 
Sinhala Sign Language (SSL) is the primary visual-gestural language of the Sri Lankan Deaf 
community. Like all natural sign languages, SSL has a distinct grammar that differs 
fundamentally from spoken Sinhala: SSL uses topic-comment sentence structure (rather than 
Sinhala's subject-object-verb order), spatial referencing to establish discourse participants in 
signing space, and a rich non-manual component in which facial expressions, head 
movements, eye gaze, and body posture carry grammatical meaning equivalent to verbal 
morphology in spoken languages. 
For the proposed system, three grammatical features of SSL are particularly relevant: (1) 
Non-Manual Markers (NMMs) — raised eyebrows indicate wh-questions; furrowed brows 
indicate yes/no questions or negation; head shake indicates negation; body lean forward 
indicates affirmation or emphasis. These must be generated by the mT5 gloss head as 
embedded NMM annotation tokens and encoded in the bone rotation output via head and 
spine rotation parameters. (2) Temporal aspect marking — SSL uses timeline gestures and 
temporal adverbs (PAST, FUTURE, NOW) rather than verbal inflection for tense; these are 
generated from the tense markers extracted by sinling in Stage 2b. (3) Classifier predicates — 
handshapes representing objects and their spatial relationships, generated as specific bone 
configurations in the right and left hand finger joint rotations. 
C.2 Justification for Bone-Level Animation 
The choice to generate animation at the bone rotation level (21 bones × 3 Euler angles per 
frame) rather than at the gloss lookup level (select a pre-recorded clip for each gloss) is 
motivated by three linguistic requirements: (1) SSL co-articulation — consecutive signs 
blend at their boundaries in natural signing, requiring continuous interpolation between sign 
poses rather than discrete concatenation of pre-recorded clips; (2) prosodic modulation — the 
amplitude, speed, and spatial extent of signs varies with prosodic stress and emotional tone in 
ways that cannot be captured by a finite library of pre-recorded clips; (3) classifier predicates 
and spatial agreement — the specific handshape and movement of classifier predicates 
depends on the spatial context established earlier in the utterance, requiring generative rather 
than retrieved motion parameters. 
The bone-level approach generates each sign as a temporal sequence of 3D joint rotations 
from the unified mT5 transformer, enabling co-articulation through continuous interpolation 
(slerp), prosodic modulation through bone amplitude scaling, and novel classifier predicate 
generation through learned generalization from training examples.
